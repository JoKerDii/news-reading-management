### 2. [Why Cash Rewards Don't Always Produce Better Ideas](https://hbr.org/2026/09/why-cash-rewards-dont-always-produce-better-ideas)

**Published:** September–October 2026 | **By:** HBR Editors · HBR Magazine — Sep/Oct 2026

**Summary:** Companies often assume paying for inventions yields more innovation, but research on inventors at publicly listed Chinese firms (2,376 inventor-year observations) finds the same incentive produces different outcomes depending on professional identity. Specialists in narrow technical fields responded with fewer but more novel and technically significant inventions, while output-focused incentives shifted what and how much others produced. The lesson: tailor reward programs to inventor type rather than applying uniform cash incentives.

### 5. [AI Is Revolutionizing Strategic Decision-Making](https://hbr.org/2026/09/ai-is-revolutionizing-strategic-decision-making)

**Published:** September–October 2026 | **By:** Felipe A. Csaszar · HBR Magazine — Sep/Oct 2026

**Summary:** Strategy tools have historically been simple because human time and cognition are finite. AI removes those constraints: it can generate and evaluate thousands of strategic options, maintain continuously updated views of markets and competitors, and stress-test plans through structured debate free of internal politics. Because these tools are available to everyone, lasting advantage will come from pairing them with proprietary data, integrated workflows, and faster execution.

### 6. [Transformation Should Be a Learning Journey](https://hbr.org/2026/09/transformation-should-be-a-learning-journey)

**Published:** September–October 2026 | **By:** Evgeny Kaganer, Christoph Loch · HBR Magazine — Sep/Oct 2026

**Summary:** Most transformations fail because they are run as rigid top-down programs whose predefined goals and timelines become obsolete as conditions shift. Drawing on research into large-scale change efforts and a contrast between DBS Bank and GE, the authors argue transformation should be treated as an evolving learning journey. ### 7. [Collaborate on the Core. Compete on the Edges.](https://hbr.org/2026/09/collaborate-on-the-core-compete-on-the-edges)

**Published:** September–October 2026 | **By:** Frank Nagle · HBR Magazine — Sep/Oct 2026

**Summary:** Companies win neither by isolating themselves nor by overcollaborating; the advantage lies in knowing what to build with rivals and what to keep proprietary. Firms should collaborate on the "core" — infrastructure, standards, and trust that let an ecosystem function — and compete on the "edges" where differentiation happens. A five-factor framework (market dynamics, technology life-cycle stage, position in the stack, competition level, and social acceptance/regulation) guides the choice.

### 8. [How AI Agents Orchestrate Work Across Silos](https://hbr.org/2026/09/how-ai-agents-orchestrate-work-across-silos)

**Published:** September–October 2026 | **By:** Kris Johnson Ferreira, Jordan Tong · HBR Magazine — Sep/Oct 2026

**Summary:** Many companies apply AI to individual tasks but struggle with the cross-functional work that determines enterprise performance. Based on field research at Walmart, Amazon, Ericsson, Ramp, and Medtronic, the authors describe agentic AI orchestration: systems that link task-based AI tools across workflows, perform analyses, route information, and surface trade-offs, while humans supply context and tacit knowledge, set guardrails, and make final calls. Redesigning decisions around this collaboration speeds response.

### 9. [Don't Underestimate the Power of Customer Referrals](https://hbr.org/2026/09/dont-underestimate-the-power-of-customer-referrals)

**Published:** September–October 2026 | **By:** Fred Reichheld, Jamie Cleghorn, Wojtek Kokoszka · HBR Magazine — Sep/Oct 2026

**Summary:** An analysis of more than 10 million consumers finds that referred customers — about 20% of new customers — generate 72% of the profits from all new customers, costing less to acquire while staying longer, buying more, and referring other high-value customers. Most firms miss this because attribution systems overcredit paid channels and undercount word of mouth. Leaders should treat referrals as a growth metric, identify true promoters, and design experiences that inspire recommendations.

**Summary:** CEO success depends heavily on deliberately managing a board relationship that evolves through four predictable stages: building trust and understanding early, shaping the board into a strategic partner as credibility grows, guarding against complacency once trust is established, and finally focusing on succession and long-term governance. ### 11. [How Much Should You Be Investing in Growth?](https://hbr.org/2026/09/how-much-should-you-be-investing-in-growth)

**Published:** September–October 2026 | **By:** Paul Blase, Paul Leinwand · HBR Magazine — Sep/Oct 2026

**Summary:** Analyzing a decade of performance data on 2,900 U.S. public companies, the authors identify an "investment sweet spot" where balancing asset growth against return on assets maximizes valuation multiples. The sweet spot differs for accelerating, steady-growth, and mature industries, and firms operating outside their optimal range suffer valuation penalties of 20% to 70%. Effective capital allocation requires managing investments as an integrated portfolio aligned with strategic priorities and clear growth logic.

### 12. [5 Ways to Wield Influence When You Lack Authority](https://hbr.org/2026/09/5-ways-to-wield-influence-when-you-lack-authority)

**Published:** September–October 2026 | **By:** Pamela Meyer · HBR Magazine — Sep/Oct 2026

**Summary:** As formal authority loses effectiveness in modern organizations, leaders must influence outcomes they don't control. Meyer outlines five tactics: identifying the hidden power brokers who actually shape decisions, using discreet back channels to defuse conflict and build alignment, timing interventions to individual and organizational readiness, cultivating networks of problem-solving connections, and reading emotional and social cues to surface unspoken concerns. The common thread is creating conditions in which others choose to act rather than feel compelled.
