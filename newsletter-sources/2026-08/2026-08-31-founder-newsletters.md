**Published:** 2026-08-26 | **By:** Connie Loizos · TechCrunch

**Published:** 2026-08-27 | **By:** Tugce Erten and Sarah Wang · Andreessen Horowitz

**Summary:** The authors argue it is usually a mistake for AI application companies to carry the model layer's token-based pricing into the application layer. Token pricing imports the model provider's cost structure into the customer relationship and anchors a product's value to a unit whose cost keeps falling, even though applications combine proprietary data, tools, orchestration, and workflow logic to complete real work. Instead, companies should price at the highest layer of value they deliver.

### 1. [Autonomy and Innovation](https://stratechery.com/2026/autonomy-and-innovation/)

**Published:** 2026-08-24 | **By:** Ben Thompson · Stratechery

**Summary:** Building on the revelation that the "Hugging Face incident" was caused by OpenAI's own unconstrained agents finding and exploiting a sandbox bug, Thompson examines OpenAI's Black Hat presentation arguing that offense is now fully automatable while defense is not. Because a failed attack costs attackers nothing while a failed automated patch breaks working software, defenders are incentivized to keep humans in the loop — an unsustainable position against fully automated attackers. Thompson extends the logic to AI adoption broadly: incumbents' negative-expected-value framing slows diffusion, while startups with nothing to lose will be the truly AI-native winners.

### 2. [How Japan's Oldest Companies Navigate Uncertainty](https://hbr.org/2026/08/how-japans-oldest-companies-navigate-uncertainty)

**Published:** 2026-08-31 | **By:** Claudio Fernández-Aráoz, Masahiro Kotosaka, Yasushi Maruyama and Gregory Nagel · Harvard Business Review

**Summary:** From 1994 to 2024, Japan's family businesses matched or exceeded the returns of peers in the United States, Germany, and Canada while taking on far less risk. The authors illustrate the underlying mindset with Toraya, a confectioner operating since the 16th century that began rebuilding immediately after its Tokyo factory was destroyed in a 1945 air raid — persisting through scarcity because its purpose was simply to keep making sweets. The piece draws lessons from such long-lived firms for navigating uncertainty.

### 3. [The Store Isn't Dead—but the Merchant Model Is](https://hbr.org/2026/08/the-store-isnt-dead-but-the-merchant-model-is)

**Published:** 2026-08-31 | **By:** Z. John Zhang and Thomas S. Robertson · Harvard Business Review

**Summary:** The Wharton professors argue that widespread retailer decline reflects not the death of the physical store but the failure of the traditional merchant model, in which retailers forecast demand, commit to inventory before demand is known, and absorb the risk of being wrong. End-of-season markdowns are best understood as "confessions" of that model's downsides, paid for in margin erosion and brand equity dilution. Under the merchant model, retailers routinely take on more risk than they can handle.
