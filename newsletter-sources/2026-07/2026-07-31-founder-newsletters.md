## Harvard Business Review

### 60. [How Agentic AI Supercharges Startups and Threatens Incumbents](https://hbr.org/2026/07/how-agentic-ai-supercharges-startups-and-threatens-incumbents)

**Published:** 2026-07-01 | **By:** Vivian S. Lee, Linda Mantia and Jon McNeill · HBR Magazine — July–August 2026

**Summary:** The authors argue that startups deploying coordinated systems of AI agents that plan, act and adapt autonomously are compressing the time, capital and headcount needed to launch and scale a company, with products that once took large teams over a year now built in weeks by a handful of people. AI-native startups accumulate proprietary workflow knowledge through agent use, creating compounding advantages. Incumbents face a structural disadvantage from siloed data, legacy workflows and rigid roles, and the authors recommend redesigning processes before automating, improving data quality, and clarifying human-AI handoffs.

### 61. [AI Is Changing How Customers Choose Your Business](https://hbr.org/2026/07/ai-is-changing-how-customers-choose-your-business)

**Published:** 2026-07-06 | **By:** Graham Kenny and Ganna Pogrebna · Harvard Business Review

**Summary:** As AI increasingly mediates how customers research and evaluate suppliers, the authors argue competitive advantage shifts from direct customer understanding to managing AI-shaped interactions. They draw on three small and mid-sized businesses: a manufacturer that screened AI-generated inquiries before committing engineering time to quotations, a boutique hotel that monitored and corrected how AI tools described it, and a B2B software company that replaced periodic customer reviews with continuous monitoring of feedback and AI-generated market perceptions. The common lesson is to treat AI as a new intermediary in the customer relationship.

### 62. [You Outsourced the AI—but You Still Own the Risk](https://hbr.org/2026/07/you-outsourced-the-ai-but-you-still-own-the-risk)

**Published:** 2026-07-09 | **By:** M. Alejandra Parra-Orlandoni and Paulo Carvão · Harvard Business Review

**Summary:** The authors note that courts and regulators increasingly hold enterprises responsible when third-party AI systems they deploy discriminate, mishandle data or harm customers, citing suits involving Peloton, iTutorGroup, Workday and Cigna. Accountability tends to fall on the organization closest to the end user rather than the model provider. They identify four under-managed exposures — opacity in upstream models, liability triggered by customization, dependence on hard-to-replace vendors, and fragmented regulatory demands — and recommend contractual transparency, formal governance of customization, designing for portability, and anchoring compliance in frameworks such as NIST AI RMF or ISO/IEC 42001.

### 63. [Research: How AI Agents Broaden the Scope of Knowledge Work](https://hbr.org/2026/07/research-how-ai-agents-broaden-the-scope-of-knowledge-work)

**Published:** 2026-07-29 | **By:** Jeremy Yang, Kate Zyskowski, Noah Yonack and Jerry Ma · Harvard Business Review

**Summary:** In a field study comparing Perplexity's conversational assistant with its autonomous agent, the agent performed more work autonomously, cut task time, lowered cost and left users more satisfied. It also expanded the scope of work people attempted, crossing occupational boundaries and prompting more complex multi-step projects that rarely appeared with a standard assistant. The authors conclude that as workers shift from operators to supervisors, leaders must redesign roles, workflows and oversight to govern the expanded autonomy.

### 65. [AI and the Looming Competition for Margin](https://hbr.org/2026/07/ai-and-the-looming-competition-for-margin)

**Published:** 2026-07-30 | **By:** Philipp Carlsson-Szlezak and Paul Swartz · Harvard Business Review

**Summary:** The authors argue that new technologies including AI deliver productivity growth in one of three ways: reducing input costs, growing outputs with the same inputs, or creating an entirely new business model. Each may confer temporary advantage, but all converge on the same end state of eroding margin. They conclude that economically transformative technologies are inherently deflationary and that consumers, not firms, are the durable winners.
